# The twenty styles

Each one: what it actually is, when it works, when it fails, and the tell that separates
a real execution from a costume.

---

## 1. Minimalism

Not "less stuff". What survives removal. Every element left has to justify itself.

**Type** One family, two weights, a strict scale. **Colour** Two or three, with a ratio
like 90/8/2. **Space** The primary material. **Motion** Short, few, precise.

**Works for** product UI, premium retail, deep tech, anything where the content is the
point.
**Fails when** there is not enough content to carry it. Minimalism with thin content is
an empty room, and it exposes weak copy instantly.
**The tell** Real minimalism has one deliberate moment of tension - a scale jump, a lone
accent, an asymmetry. Without it you have blankness, not restraint.

## 2. Maximalism

Abundance as the message. Not clutter - it needs a stronger underlying grid than
minimalism does, because it is holding more.

**Type** Multiple families, extreme scale contrast, type as image. **Colour** Many, but
governed - a fixed palette used loudly beats free-for-all. **Motion** Layered, but one
thing leads.

**Works for** culture, fashion, festivals, editorial, brands whose product is energy.
**Fails when** there is a job to be done on the page. Nobody completes a checkout in a
maximalist interface.
**The tell** Good maximalism has a clear reading order. Bad maximalism has none, and you
can see people's eyes not knowing where to go.

## 3. Futuristic

Only works when it is a *specific* future. Whose future, in what year, with what
assumptions. "The future" in general is 2014's idea of it: blue glow and hexagons.

**Type** Technical, often mono or a grotesque with tight tracking. **Colour** Restrained
base with one signal colour. **Texture** Data, measurement, instrumentation.
**Motion** Mechanical precision or organic systems, pick one.

**Works for** deep tech, AI infrastructure, mobility, space.
**Fails when** it borrows from science fiction instead of from science. Sci-fi UI is
designed to look good on camera, not to work.
**The tell** Real futuristic is borrowed from instrumentation - scientific publishing,
telemetry, CAD. Costume futuristic is borrowed from film.

## 4. Vector art

Flat shape, hard edge, no rendering. Built from geometry, so it scales and stays
consistent across a system.

**Type** Geometric sans usually. **Colour** Flat fills, limited palette, no gradients
unless the whole system uses them. **Construction** Consistent stroke weight, consistent
corner radius, one perspective.

**Works for** explainers, brand illustration, icon systems, technical diagrams.
**Fails when** every character has the same posture and the same round head - the
corporate illustration look that reads as a template.
**The tell** Consistency of construction rules. If two illustrations disagree about
stroke weight or radius, there is no system.

## 5. Collage art

Fragments in tension - photography, texture, type, cut edges. The seams are the point.

**Type** Often found or mismatched, deliberately. **Colour** Comes from the sources,
unified with a grade or a halftone. **Texture** Paper, tape, photocopy, torn edge.

**Works for** culture, music, zines, campaigns, anything with a counter-cultural claim.
**Fails when** the fragments have nothing to do with each other. Collage needs an
argument between the pieces.
**The tell** Real collage has physical logic - things overlap, cast shadows, sit at
angles a hand would produce. Digital collage with everything at 0 degrees is a moodboard.

## 6. Retro

One decade, executed precisely. 1950s American advertising and 1970s Italian editorial
are not the same style and mixing them is the failure mode.

**Type** Period-accurate. This matters more than anything else.
**Colour** Period-accurate, including the limitations - limited ink counts, specific
process colours.
**Texture** The printing process of the era - halftone, misregistration, paper stock.

**Works for** food and drink, hospitality, heritage brands, anything selling warmth.
**Fails when** the reference is "vintage" generally. That is a filter, not a style.
**The tell** Get the printing constraint right. 1960s work looks that way partly because
of what four-colour offset could and could not do.

## 7. Cyberpunk

High tech, low life. The tech is everywhere and it is dirty, cheap and failing. It is a
political style, not a neon filter.

**Type** Mono, condensed, often multilingual layering. **Colour** Dark base, two
saturated signals, usually a warm and a cool. **Texture** Scanlines, compression
artefacts, wear, signage.

**Works for** games, security products, crypto, nightlife.
**Fails when** it is used by an optimistic brand. Cyberpunk says the system is broken -
that is a strange claim to make about your own product.
**The tell** Decay. Clean cyberpunk is just neon.

## 8. Pop art

Mass production as subject matter. Repetition, commercial printing, flat bold colour,
the everyday object elevated.

**Type** Bold, commercial, often condensed. **Colour** Primary and saturated, flat, with
visible process - Ben Day dots, misregistration. **Composition** Repetition and framing.

**Works for** consumer goods, merch, youth brands, food.
**Fails when** it is "bright colours". Pop art has a subject and a comment on it.
**The tell** The comment. Without it, it is decoration in primary colours.

## 9. Glassmorphism

Translucency and blur to build depth and layer hierarchy. An OS idiom before it was a
web trend.

**Type** Clean sans, lighter weights survive on glass. **Colour** Needs a background
with something worth blurring. **Depth** Layer order must be consistent, with one light
source.

**Works for** OS-adjacent interfaces, overlays, dashboards with a spatial model.
**Fails when** there is nothing behind the glass, or when text sits on it and the
contrast collapses as the background scrolls.
**The tell** Contrast tested against the *worst* background the glass can sit over, not
the hero image it was designed on.

## 10. Clay style

Soft, matte, rounded three-dimensional forms with weight. Friendly and physical.

**Type** Rounded or soft-cornered geometric sans. **Colour** Pastel to mid, low
saturation, soft shadows. **Motion** This is where it lives - squash, settle, spring.
Clay that does not move with weight is just a rounded rectangle.

**Works for** onboarding, children's products, fintech that wants to feel safe,
explainer illustration.
**Fails when** the product is serious or expensive. Clay makes things feel approachable
and inexpensive, which is sometimes the opposite of what is needed.
**The tell** Consistent light direction and material. Two objects lit differently breaks
it instantly.

## 11. Pixel art

Resolution as a constraint, honoured. A fixed grid, a fixed palette, and every pixel
placed.

**Type** Bitmap type at its native size only. Never scaled non-integer.
**Colour** Small palette, often period-accurate to a console or a display.
**Scale** Integer scaling only. A 3.5x scaled sprite is the whole failure.

**Works for** games, developer tools, retro computing, anything with a craft claim.
**Fails when** mixed with high-resolution elements without a reason.
**The tell** Integer scaling and a consistent pixel grid across every asset.

## 12. Editorial

Type-led, print discipline applied anywhere. Hierarchy through typography rather than
through boxes and colour.

**Type** A display face and a text face, a real scale, and attention to measure, rag and
widows. **Colour** Usually minimal, so type does the work. **Layout** A grid with
deliberate breaks. Generous margins.

**Works for** magazines, long-form, considered brands, anything selling judgement.
**Fails when** the content is not worth reading. Editorial design promises substance.
**The tell** Measure and rag. 65 characters, no widows, balanced headings. If those are
wrong, the rest is decoration.

## 13. Y2K

1998 to 2003 optimistic digital. Chrome, bubble forms, lens flare, translucent plastic,
early-web gradients. Cheerful and slightly naive.

**Type** Rounded techno, chrome effects, tight tracking. **Colour** Silver, ice blue,
lime, hot pink. **Texture** Plastic, chrome, star sparkles, low-res gradients.

**Works for** fashion, music, consumer app launches aimed young.
**Fails when** the audience did not live through it or the brand needs to seem stable.
**The tell** The optimism. Y2K was pre-cynicism. Ironic Y2K reads as a costume.

## 14. Swiss design

International Typographic Style. Grid, objective photography, a neutral grotesque,
asymmetric layout, information over expression.

**Type** Helvetica, Univers, Akzidenz, or a modern equivalent. Few sizes, few weights.
**Colour** Usually black, white and one. **Layout** A visible, obeyed grid.
**Motion** Precise and brief, or none.

**Works for** institutions, finance, technical products, wayfinding, anything where
credibility outranks personality.
**Fails when** the brand needs warmth or distinctiveness. Swiss done well is
authoritative and slightly cold, which is often exactly right and occasionally fatal.
**The tell** The grid has to be real and obeyed, including in the awkward places. Swiss
with an off-grid element is not Swiss.

## 15. Surreal design

The impossible rendered plausibly. Scale violations, impossible physics, unexpected
juxtaposition - executed with total realism, because the realism is what makes it land.

**Type** Usually restrained, so the image carries it. **Imagery** Photographic or
photorealistic, consistent light. **Composition** One impossible thing, not five.

**Works for** campaigns, culture, luxury, AI products where the promise is genuinely
strange.
**Fails when** the impossibility is arbitrary. Surrealism needs to mean something or it
is a stock generation.
**The tell** Rendering quality and lighting consistency. Sloppy surrealism just looks
like an error.

## 16. Bohemian

Handmade, earthy, imperfect. Natural materials, organic shape, warm neutrals, visible
craft.

**Type** Humanist serif or a hand-drawn face, often mixed with a simple sans.
**Colour** Terracotta, sand, sage, ochre. Muted. **Texture** Linen, clay, paper, plant.

**Works for** wellness, craft goods, hospitality, slow fashion.
**Fails when** the product is technical or mass-produced. Also: this is the default for
wellness, so it differentiates nothing in that category.
**The tell** Real imperfection, not an imperfection filter. Hand-drawn elements that are
actually hand-drawn.

## 17. Victorian style

Ornament, density, hierarchy through decoration. Engraving, flourish, many typefaces on
one surface, arranged with strict symmetry.

**Type** Multiple display faces, ornamental, often condensed. **Colour** Deep, limited,
often with gold or a single accent. **Texture** Engraving, letterpress, aged paper.

**Works for** spirits, apothecary, heritage brands, publishing, anything selling age.
**Fails when** it needs to work small or on screen at speed. Victorian is a large-format
style.
**The tell** Symmetry and density done correctly. It looks chaotic but it is rigidly
ordered, and that order is what makes it read as craft rather than clip art.

## 18. Graffiti

Mark-making, territory, speed. Letterform as the whole subject. Layered, illegal-feeling,
made with a specific tool.

**Type** Hand-drawn letterform, not a graffiti font. That is the single biggest tell.
**Colour** High contrast, spray-can palette. **Texture** Spray overspray, drips, surface.

**Works for** streetwear, sport, music, youth brands.
**Fails when** the brand has no claim to the culture. This style has an origin and
borrowing it carelessly is noticed.
**The tell** Real letterform construction. A downloaded font over a brick texture fools
nobody who knows.

## 19. Aurora

Soft luminous gradients, blurred colour fields, glow. Calm, modern, weightless.

**Type** Clean geometric or neo-grotesque sans. **Colour** Two to four blending, low
contrast between them, high contrast to the text. **Motion** Slow drift.

**Works for** SaaS, AI products, wellness tech, anything wanting calm.
**Fails when** everything else in the category has already done it - which is now the
case. Aurora is the current default, which means choosing it is choosing to look like
everyone.
**The tell** Text contrast over the gradient's lightest point, and a gradient with an
actual colour story rather than purple to blue.

## 20. Handwritten

The human hand present. Lettering, annotation, marginalia, signature.

**Type** Real handwriting or a well-made script, used sparingly against a neutral text
face. **Colour** Usually minimal - ink on paper. **Texture** Paper, pen pressure,
correction.

**Works for** personal brands, greetings, food, craft, anything where the maker matters.
**Fails when** used for body copy, or when the "handwriting" is a font used at every
size. One hand, used like a hand - a few words, placed where a person would place them.
**The tell** Variation. Real handwriting never repeats a letterform identically. A font
that does is visible immediately.

---

## Combining

The only safe combination is a disciplined base plus one accent move:

- Swiss base, one handwritten annotation
- Editorial base, one collage moment
- Minimal base, clay physics in motion only
- Swiss base, one surreal image

Name both parts and say which is the base. If you cannot name which is the base, you
have two styles and therefore none.
